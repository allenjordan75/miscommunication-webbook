(Mis)Communication: An (Un)Commonsense Approach to Interpersonal Communication

Jordan Allen, Ph.D.
Opening chapter
Chapter0

(Un)Knowing Communication

Why Knowing Less Can Help More

Chapter Overview

In this opening chapter, Professor Allen introduces the strange problem at the center of the book: we have more communication advice, training, technology, and vocabulary than ever, yet communicating often still feels confusing, risky, and weirdly hard.

Students will preview two frameworks that shape the whole textbook. The knowing framework treats communication as information to collect, transfer, correct, or apply. The understanding framework treats communicating as contextual sense-making: figuring out how facts, people, relationships, histories, and meanings connect while interaction is still unfolding.

This chapter will prepare you to:

  • Explain the communication paradox and why it matters for an interpersonal communication course.
  • Compare and contrast knowing communication with understanding communicating.
  • Describe how propositional success, monologic thinking, the certainty trap, communication myths, and brittle knowledge can shape communication advice.
  • Use objectual understanding, dialectical thinking, productive confusion, sense-making, and judgment to explain why communicating requires more than rules or scripts.

1Introduction: The Communication Paradox

I hope you finish this course knowing less than when you started.

I know. That is a suspicious sentence to put at the beginning of a textbook. It is the kind of sentence that could get me tossed into author-and-professor prison, where I will be punished with dull pencils, dead dry erase markers, a permanently jammed copier, and textbooks that define communication as “the process of sending and receiving messages” as if human beings have ever been that tidy.

A brutal punishment.

Because, after all, isn’t the point of a class to help you know more? Isn’t that what textbooks are for? Concepts. Vocabulary. Diagrams. Bolded key terms. A figure with arrows moving from “sender” to “receiver,” then back again, as if communication were a polite game of catch between two emotionally regulated people with excellent posture.

But here is the thing. I am not asking you to learn nothing.

You have an idea. You package it into words. You send it. Someone receives it. Maybe there is “noise,” because every communication model needs one little chaos goblin. Then feedback happens. Communication complete.

Beautiful. Not useless. But also wildly incomplete.

I do want you to learn things in this class. I want you to learn communication theory. I want you to learn useful vocabulary. I want you to have names for things you have experienced but maybe have never had language for. I want you to be able to read research, question advice, evaluate claims, and think more carefully about the relationships and interactions that make up your life.

So no, I am not anti-knowledge. That would be a strange career choice.

What I mean is that I hope you leave this course less certain about some of the things you thought you already knew about communication. I hope you become less satisfied with advice that sounds clean but falls apart in actual relationships. I hope you become less impressed by rules that pretend people are simpler than they are. I hope you stop treating communication as something you can master by collecting enough tips, scripts, definitions, hacks, and facts.

And I hope, on the other side of that, you understand communicating more.

That is the risky little project of this book. Not just knowing communication. Understanding communicating.

1.1The Communication Paradox

So why start with that strange little wish? Because the first puzzle of this book is also strange: we have never had more communication advice, communication training, communication technology, and communication vocabulary. And yet many people still feel like beginners when the moment actually asks them to communicate.

We spend real money trying to get better at this. Training Magazine reported that U.S. training expenditures reached $102.8 billion in 2025, including $16 billion in outside products and services (Freifeld, 2025). Not all of that is communication training, obviously. Some of it is technical training, compliance training, safety training, and the kind of online module that makes employees stare at a screen wondering whether the “next” button will ever become available. But communication-adjacent training is everywhere in that world: leadership, feedback, teamwork, conflict, emotional intelligence, coaching, culture, customer service, professional writing, and now AI-assisted communication.

And still, people do not exactly seem relaxed about communicating. Pew Research Center reported in 2025 that Americans trust one another less than they did a few decades ago, and in one 2025 survey, 61% of U.S. adults said their trust in other people had decreased in recent years (Pew Research Center, 2025). AXA’s 2025 research found that 71% of UK young adults ages 18 to 28 said they struggle to maintain concentration during in-person conversations, and 63% said they struggle with real-life interaction (AXA UK, 2025).

Together, those patterns point toward a tension worth taking seriously: we have more communication language, training, and tools than ever, while actual communicating still feels loaded, uncertain, and hard to trust.

That is the communication paradox: we spend massive amounts of time, money, education, training, technology, and cultural energy on communication, while many people still feel uncertain, anxious, stuck, or ineffective when they actually have to communicate.

Key Term: Communication Paradox

Communication paradox: the tension between how much attention we give communication and how uncertain, anxious, or stuck people still feel when they actually communicate.

In simpler language: we know more about communication than ever, but communicating does not feel easier.

1.2The Problem

So here is the problem: we have often been taught to treat communication as a knowing problem when it is also, and often more importantly, an understanding problem. Not because knowing is useless. Knowing is useful. It is just not enough for the kinds of messy, relational, half-clear, context-loaded moments where communicating actually lives.

A knowing problem asks for the right fact, the right term, the right script, the right answer, the right rule. An understanding problem asks how the pieces fit together. What is happening here? What does this mean in this relationship? What history is shaping it? What assumptions are being made? What does the moment require?

That difference matters. If we keep treating communication as trivia plus scripts, we will keep producing people who know communication terms but still feel lost inside actual communicating. So the rest of this chapter gives you the map. First, we will look at the knowing framework and why it can create brittle certainty. Then we will turn to the understanding framework and why it gives us durable flexibility. Finally, we will talk about what this means for the rest of the book: this is not a textbook designed only for answer extraction. It is a textbook designed to help you practice seeing.

2The Problem with Knowing: Brittle Certainty

If knowing helped us communicate, we all would be communication experts.

That line is not an insult to knowledge. It is a warning about what happens when knowing becomes the whole framework for communication. A framework is a way of seeing. It shapes what we notice, what we ignore, what counts as evidence, and what kind of response seems reasonable. Knowing gives us facts, vocabulary, definitions, categories, and shared reference points. It helps us correct mistakes. It helps us coordinate. It helps us say, “The meeting starts at 10,” “The assignment is due Sunday,” or “Self-disclosure means revealing personal information about yourself.”

Key Term: Framework

Framework: a way of seeing that shapes what we notice, what we ignore, what counts as evidence, and what response seems reasonable.

Key Term: Knowing

Knowing: having accurate information you can state, recall, identify, or correct.

But when knowing becomes the whole framework for communication, communication begins to look more stable, correctable, transferable, and rule-based than it really is. It starts to look like a thing we can master if we collect enough accurate pieces. That is where brittle certainty begins.

2.1Definition

Jonathan Kvanvig helps us make this distinction because he argues that knowledge and understanding are not the same achievement. In his work, knowledge is often connected to propositional success. That phrase sounds like it was invented to make freshmen close the laptop, so let’s translate it. Propositional success means succeeding with a claim: having a belief or statement that is accurate, reliable, and true.

Here are simple examples. If I say, “The quiz opens at noon,” and the quiz really does open at noon, that is propositional success. If you know your roommate said, “I’m fine,” and those were the actual words they used, that is propositional success. If you know your friend canceled plans three times, and yes, the cancellations happened, that is propositional success. The claim matches the facts. Lovely. We love a fact that shows up to work on time.

Kvanvig’s point is not that knowledge is useless. It is that knowledge does not exhaust what we value when we say someone understands something (Kvanvig, 2003, 2009). You can know a fact without understanding the situation that makes the fact matter. You can know what someone said without understanding what they meant. You can know the rule without understanding when the rule should bend, pause, or be put down entirely.

Key Term: Propositional Success

Propositional success: succeeding with a claim: having a belief or statement that is accurate, reliable, and true.

Why it matters: It explains what knowing does well without mistaking accuracy for understanding.

So, translated for this class: knowing helps you hold accurate pieces of information. Understanding helps you see how those pieces connect.

Think of knowing like connecting pieces of trivia at a pub night. It is genuinely useful there. You know the capital, the lyric, the year, the actor, the weird fact about octopuses, and suddenly your table wins a plastic keychain shaped like a guitar. Beautiful. Knowing shines when the task is to retrieve and connect correct pieces of information. It is less helpful when you are trying to understand why your friend’s “I’m fine” does not sound fine, why the same fight keeps repeating, or why the “right words” still landed badly. Those moments do not ask only, “Do you have the fact?” They ask, “Can you understand what the fact means here?”

Case in Point: Pub Trivia

Knowing shines when the task is to retrieve correct pieces of information: the capital, the lyric, the year, the actor, or the odd fact. It is less helpful when a relational situation asks what a fact means here.

That transition matters because interpersonal communication usually does not fail only because someone lacks a fact. It often fails because someone has a fact and then treats that fact as the whole story.

2.2Monologic Thinking

Once knowing becomes the whole frame, it often pulls us into monologic thinking. Monologic thinking is either/or thinking and one-answer thinking. It looks for one truth, one cause, one correct interpretation, one person who is right, and one person who is wrong.

Key Term: Monologic Thinking

Monologic thinking: either/or thinking that searches for one truth, one correct interpretation, or one person who is right.

Why it matters: It can flatten messy interactions into simple winners and losers before anyone understands what the conflict means.

This kind of thinking is tempting because propositional success trains us to ask whether a claim is true or false. Did they say that? Did the text arrive? Did the meeting start at 10? Did the apology include the word “sorry”? Those questions matter. But they are not always enough.

Monologic thinking turns communicating into binaries: true or false, right or wrong, good or bad, clear or unclear, victim or villain, success or failure. Sometimes those distinctions matter. Sometimes something really is false. Sometimes something really is harmful. Sometimes we need to say, “No, that is not okay.”

But if we only think monologically, communication gets flattened. The interaction becomes a courtroom before anyone understands what the conflict is actually about. We start searching for the one absolute truth of the situation: who really meant what, who is really responsible, who is really wrong, who has the correct interpretation.

And people are rarely that flat. They are irritatingly dimensional.

Take “I was just being honest.” A monologic reading might say, “Honesty is good, so I did nothing wrong.” But communicating is rarely that tidy. You can be honest and careless. You can tell the truth and choose a cruel time. You can say something accurate and still ignore the relationship it lands inside. This is why the true/false question is sometimes too small. The message may be true. The communicating may still be a hot mess.

2.3The Emotion of Certainty

The next problem is not just intellectual. It is emotional. Certainty feels good.

The certainty trap happens when the feeling that we know makes us stop asking questions. The knowing framework is especially vulnerable to this because propositional success can feel so clean. The claim is true. The fact is accurate. The rule is familiar. The advice is easy to repeat. Case closed.

Key Term: Certainty Trap

Certainty trap: the moment when feeling like we know makes us stop asking questions.

Except, not always.

Communication is messy. People are hard to read. Relationships change. Context matters. Timing matters. Power matters. Culture matters. History matters. The same words can mean different things depending on who says them, how they say them, and what happened before. That is a lot. So we reach for rules because rules feel like relief.

Say this. Do that. Avoid this phrase. Use this script. Good communicators do this. Bad communicators do that. Certainty says, “Do not worry. Here is the answer.”

The problem is that certainty can shut down curiosity.

The Dunning-Kruger effect helps explain why this is so sticky. Kruger and Dunning found that people with lower skill in a domain may overestimate their ability because they do not yet know enough to recognize what they are missing (Kruger & Dunning, 1999; Dunning, 2011). In plain language: sometimes we are most confident before we understand the problem well enough to be appropriately humble.

Communication is especially vulnerable to this because we communicate all the time. We talk, text, listen, argue, explain, apologize, avoid, flirt, complain, comfort, and misunderstand daily. Familiarity can masquerade as understanding. Doing something often is not the same as understanding how it works. I have used a microwave for years. Please do not ask me to explain the microwave. I will embarrass all of us.

2.4Brittle Knowledge

Communication Myth

Myth: Just communicate more.

What this misses: Some communication problems are not volume problems. More words can repeat the same misunderstanding if nobody understands what the conflict means.

Better question: What would I need to understand before more communication would help?

This is where knowing can become brittle. If knowing is built around accurate pieces, then it can struggle when the pieces do not stay still. And in real communicating, the pieces almost never stay still. Tone shifts. Power matters. Timing matters. History shows up. The same sentence can land differently depending on who says it, how they say it, and what happened before.

Brittle knowledge is knowledge or advice that breaks when the context changes. It is brittle because it depends too much on the situation staying stable, complete, and exactly the way the rule imagined.

Key Term: Brittle Knowledge

Brittle knowledge: knowledge or advice that breaks when the context changes.

Take “be direct.” Useful? Sometimes, yes. If someone keeps interrupting you, directness might help: “Hey, I want to finish my thought before we move on.” Beautiful. Clear. Useful.

Case in Point: Brittle Advice

Be direct, use I-statements, and don’t go to bed angry can all help. They can also break when timing, power, exhaustion, relationship history, or context changes.

But direct with whom? A close friend? A supervisor? A parent? A child? A person who has power over your job? A person who hears directness as honesty? A person who hears directness as disrespect? A person who is already defensive? A person who is grieving? A person who asked for clarity? A person who asked for gentleness? The rule “be direct” does not answer those questions. Understanding has to.

Or take “use I-statements.” Useful sometimes. But you can put very unhelpful things into an I-statement: “I feel like you are selfish.” Technically, the sentence starts with “I feel.” Emotionally, it is wearing a fake mustache. That is not really a feeling. That is an accusation in an I-statement costume.

And then there is “don’t go to bed angry.” The rule knows something important. It knows repair matters. It knows avoidance can become a pattern. But imagine two people in a blowout fight at 2:00 a.m. They are exhausted. Their bodies are activated. Their ability to listen generously has left the building. They know the rule, so they stay up. Then they keep fighting. Then they get mean. What happened? They followed the rule. They knew the advice. But they did not understand the situation.

This is what rigid advice does. It takes one partial truth and tries to carry it everywhere. Sometimes it helps. Sometimes it breaks in your hands.

2.5Communication as Knowledge Transfer

This is how knowing shapes our view of communication. If knowing teaches us to value accurate pieces of information, then communication starts to look like the movement of those pieces from one person to another. In plain language, that means we imagine communication as information transfer. I know the quiz is due Sunday. I tell you the quiz is due Sunday. Now you know the quiz is due Sunday. Information moved. Tiny parade.

This way of thinking is often called the conduit framework because it treats communication like a pipe or channel. I put a message into words. The words travel to you. You receive the message. If the message does not arrive correctly, we assume something blocked the pipe: noise, distraction, unclear wording, bad listening.

Key Term: Conduit Model

Conduit model: a way of imagining communication as messages moving through a channel from sender to receiver.

Does that model explain some things? Absolutely. If I say the quiz opens at noon, I do not need you to explore the existential ambiguity of noon. I need you to know when the quiz opens.

But interpersonal communicating does not stop at message delivery. A technically clear message can still be relationally hollow. A person can say the “right” words and still miss the harm. A friend can send a perfectly organized paragraph and still not understand what the conflict is about. A partner can apologize with all the correct apology ingredients and still leave you thinking, “Okay, but do you understand why that hurt?”

Here is the difference. A friend says, “You never show up for me.” You say, “That is not true. I came to your birthday dinner.” You transferred a fact. The birthday dinner happened. Congratulations to the fact. But your friend may be talking about something else: I do not feel emotionally supported by you. I do not feel like I can count on you. I do not think you understand what showing up means to me.

Communication Myth

Myth: If the facts are accurate, the communication problem is solved.

What this misses: A fact can be true and still miss the relational meaning of the moment.

Different level. Different problem. Different work. And that is why we need understanding.

3Understanding as a Framework: Durable Flexibility

If the knowing framework gives us accurate pieces, the understanding framework helps us see the pattern those pieces make. This is the shift from brittle certainty to durable flexibility. Durable does not mean vague. Flexible does not mean “anything goes.” It means the framework can respond when the situation changes.

Key Term: Understanding

Understanding: grasping how pieces of information connect so a person, situation, relationship, or interaction makes more sense.

3.1Definition

Kvanvig calls one kind of understanding objectual understanding, which is a fancy way of saying we understand something when we grasp how its parts fit together (Kvanvig, 2003, 2009). He describes understanding as a kind of mastered grasp of connections: structural connections, explanatory connections, and relationships among pieces of information.

Okay, folks, let’s translate. Objectual understanding means you understand the thing, situation, or subject as a connected whole. Not just one fact. Not just one sentence. The constellation.

Key Term: Objectual Understanding

Objectual understanding: grasping a connected whole, not just one fact or sentence.

Here are some examples. You might know that your friend canceled plans three times. Objectual understanding asks what those cancellations connect to: stress, avoidance, depression, work overload, family pressure, money, time management, or a quiet attempt to create distance. You might know that your partner got quiet after dinner. Objectual understanding asks how that quiet connects to the day, the conversation before dinner, their usual stress patterns, your shared history, and what silence has meant in this relationship before.

Or think about a group project. You may know that one person did not submit their part on time. Understanding asks what that missed deadline means in the larger pattern. Are they disengaged? Overloaded? Confused? Avoiding accountability? Waiting for clearer instructions? Working two jobs? All of those possibilities call for different responses.

So understanding is not simply “more knowing.” It is a different kind of achievement. It is not just having more data points. It is grasping how the data points relate.

3.2Dialectical Thinking

If monologic thinking is one-answer thinking, dialectical thinking is both/and thinking. It means holding more than one truth at the same time, especially when those truths are in tension.

Key Term: Dialectical Thinking

Dialectical thinking: both/and thinking that holds more than one truth at the same time, especially when those truths are in tension.

You can love someone and need space. You can be honest and still be incomplete. You can communicate clearly and still be misunderstood. You can have good intentions and still hurt someone. You can know someone well and still be surprised by them. A rule can be useful and incomplete. A person can be responsible for their impact even if their intent was good. That last one is a doozy.

Dialectical thinking does not give us an easy answer. It gives us more room. And sometimes more room is what we need before we can understand what is happening.

For example, a roommate might say, “I don’t care where we eat,” and then seem annoyed at every option. A monologic reading might say: they lied. A dialectical reading makes more room: maybe they do not care in theory, but they are tired; maybe they want you to choose, but they also have preferences; maybe they are trying to be easygoing and discovering, inconveniently, that they are not actually easygoing today. Does that make sense? The point is not to excuse everything. The point is to keep the situation dimensional long enough to interpret it well.

3.3The Posture of Uncertainty

The knowing framework often reaches for certainty. The understanding framework asks us to tolerate uncertainty long enough to learn from it.

That is where productive confusion comes in. Productive confusion is the discomfort that happens when an old certainty stops working before a new understanding has fully formed. It is that irritated little moment when you think, “Wait. I thought being direct was good. Now you are telling me directness can be helpful or harmful depending on context?”

Key Term: Productive Confusion

Productive confusion: the discomfort that happens when an old certainty stops working before a new understanding has fully formed.

Yes. Annoying, right?

But that irritation is not always failure. Sometimes it is the sound of an old rule losing its grip. Productive confusion asks you to stay with the mess long enough to see what the old rule was hiding.

Here are more ordinary examples. You may feel productive confusion when you realize “I was just being honest” does not automatically make honesty kind. You may feel it when “setting a boundary” starts to look suspiciously like avoiding a conversation. You may feel it when “listen more” does not help because the problem is not the amount of listening but the meaning each person is making from what is being heard.

This posture matters because understanding is not built by rushing to closure. It is built by asking better questions before the first answer gets too comfortable. It asks, “What else might be true?” “What am I assuming?” “What context am I missing?” “What would change if I looked at this from the other person’s side of the interaction?”

3.4Structural Flexibility

Understanding is durable because it is flexible. Again, not flexible as in “nothing means anything.” Flexible as in able to adapt while staying responsible to the situation.

Where brittle knowledge depends on the rule staying stable, understanding can adjust because it grasps relationships. If the context changes, understanding asks what changed and why it matters. If the person responds differently than expected, understanding does not immediately panic or force the old rule harder. It looks for connections.

That is what makes understanding useful in actual relationships. A partner’s silence might mean withdrawal in one context, regulation in another, exhaustion in another, and “I need ten minutes so I do not say something cruel” in another. A friend’s joke might be care, avoidance, discomfort, or a bid for connection. A coworker’s short email might be anger, efficiency, stress, or just someone typing with one hand while eating a granola bar over a keyboard like a workplace raccoon.

Understanding asks you to adapt to the person’s unique logic. What patterns do they have? What history shapes this? What is this behavior connected to? What else might be true? That kind of flexibility is not weakness. It is durability.

3.5Communication as Sense-making

The knowing framework treats communication as message transfer. The understanding framework treats communicating as sense-making: the process of figuring out what something means while you are still inside the interaction.

Key Term: Sense-making

Sense-making: figuring out what something means while you are still inside the interaction.

Let’s take a tiny sentence: “I’m fine.” The words are clear. The sentence is short. The message was sent. The message was received. So, if communication is just information transfer, we are done. They said they are fine. You now know they are fine. Pack it up. The humans have successfully exchanged language.

Except, probably not.

Because “I’m fine” can mean many different things. It can mean “I am actually fine.” It can mean “I am not fine, but I do not want to talk about it.” It can mean “I am not fine, and I desperately want you to notice.” It can mean “I am trying not to cry in this Chili’s.” It can mean “I am angry, but I am not ready to say why.” It can mean “I want space.” It can mean “I want comfort.” It can mean “I want you to ask again, but differently.”

Same words. Different meanings. So the question is not only, “What did they say?” The question is, “What does this mean here?” Here, in this relationship. Here, after this conflict. Here, with this tone. Here, with this history. Here, with this person who always says “fine” when they are one minor inconvenience away from collapse.

Sense-making also shows up in tiny digital moments. When someone texts “K,” you do not get their face. You do not get their posture. You do not know whether they are annoyed, distracted, busy, overwhelmed, or simply a person who uses letters with reckless emotional consequences. So you fill in the blank. That filling-in is sense-making. The question is whether you do it carefully, or whether you let anxiety write the whole script.

That is why understanding communicating is not just about knowing more terms. It is about practicing the kind of attention that helps us make better sense of ambiguous moments.

4From Knowing to Understanding

So what does all of this mean for the rest of the book? It means this textbook is not only trying to give you communication knowledge. It is trying to help you practice seeing.

4.1The Mirror vs. The Lens

A traditional textbook often works like a mirror that reflects an idealized version of communication back to you: clean model, neat definition, tidy diagram, calm example, correct answer. Useful sometimes. But also suspiciously well-behaved.

This book is trying to work more like a lens. A lens changes what you can see. It does not hand you a perfect answer and announce, “Here is reality.” It helps you notice relationships, tensions, assumptions, patterns, and possibilities that may have been invisible before.

That is why this book may not always sound like a traditional textbook. A textbook written mostly for knowing often tries to sound detached, like the author is floating above the subject in a clean white lab coat, untouched by the mess of actual human interaction. I am not going to do that all the time. Partly because I do not own a clean white lab coat. Mostly because communication is not something we study from nowhere.

We study it as people who have communicated badly. Been misunderstood. Misunderstood others. Over-explained. Under-explained. Avoided the conversation. Sent the text. Regretted the text. Composed the perfect response twelve hours too late. Said “I’m fine” when we were absolutely not fine. Asked “Are you mad?” in a way that made someone mad.

That does not disqualify us from studying communication. It is why communication is worth studying.

4.2Parenthetical Disambiguation

Parenthetical disambiguation is the practice of using parentheses to slow down a word that looks simple and ask what else it might mean. It is one of the ways this textbook practices both/and thinking on the page.

For example, when this book says clear (clear to whom?), direct (direct under what conditions?), professional (according to whose norms?), or effective (effective for what purpose?), the parentheses are not just decoration. They are a little pause button. They hold two things at once: the word people usually use and the complication the word hides.

Key Term: Parenthetical Disambiguation

Parenthetical disambiguation uses parentheses to slow down familiar words and ask what else they might mean: clear (clear to whom?), direct (under what conditions?), professional (according to whose norms?), effective (for what purpose?).

That is dialectical thinking in miniature. The word can be useful and incomplete. Familiar and slippery. Obvious and not obvious at all. The parentheses keep the thought from closing too quickly, which is exactly the work of understanding.

4.3Shifting from Advice to Heuristics

Communication charlatans are people or platforms that sell communication certainty without communication judgment. They promise that one phrase, one script, one body-language trick, or one “science-backed” hack will let you read people, win arguments, control impressions, or fix relationships without doing the harder work of understanding the situation. In other words: airport-bookstore witchcraft wearing a blazer.

Key Term: Communication Charlatans

Communication charlatans: people or platforms that sell easy communication certainty without the judgment needed to understand actual situations.

Communication Myth: Communication Charlatans

Communication charlatans sell certainty without judgment. They promise one phrase, script, body-language trick, or “science-backed” hack can replace understanding the situation.

This book will give you something different: heuristics. That is a fancy word for useful guides or tools for thinking. A heuristic is not a law. It is not a commandment. It is more like a flashlight. It helps you see, but it does not become the whole room.

Key Term: Heuristic

Heuristic: a flexible guide or tool for thinking, not a universal rule or script.

So when this book gives you a concept, do not ask only, “How do I memorize this?” Ask, “What does this help me notice?” When it gives you advice, do not ask only, “Is this right or wrong?” Ask, “When would this help? When would it fail? What would I need to understand before using it well?”

4.4Confronting Myths as Taken-for-Granted Knowledge

Every chapter in this book will spend time with communication myths. But we are not just going to debunk them as if the goal is to replace one simple answer with another simple answer. That would be very on-brand for knowing, and we are trying not to accidentally redecorate the same room.

Key Term: Communication Myth

Communication myth: a repeated communication belief that contains a partial truth but becomes misleading when treated as universal or complete.

Instead, we will confront myths as taken-for-granted knowledge. We will ask what partial truth the myth contains, what cultural logic keeps it alive, what it helps us notice, and what it hides.

“Communication is 93% nonverbal,” for example, is not just a random bad fact floating around. It survives because people have experienced moments where tone, face, gesture, and timing mattered more than words. The problem is not that nonverbal communication is unimportant. The problem is that a number can travel without its context, harden into certainty, and make people think they understand communication when they only know a slogan.

Don’t Flatten This

The problem with “93% nonverbal” is not that nonverbal communication is unimportant. The problem is that a number can travel without its context and harden into certainty.

4.5Moving Forward

Moving forward, this course is asking you to practice seeing. That means noticing patterns, asking better questions, checking assumptions, looking for context, holding more than one possibility, using research without worshiping it, taking advice seriously without treating it like a universal law, being willing to revise your interpretation, and seeing communication as something people do together, not something one person perfectly sends and another person perfectly receives.

That is harder than memorizing. It is also more useful. Because your life will not hand you clean communication quizzes. It will hand you weird texts, tense silences, family patterns, friendship ruptures, workplace misunderstandings, awkward apologies, romantic ambiguity, group project chaos, and people who say “nothing’s wrong” while communicating with their entire face that something is absolutely wrong.

And in those moments, knowing may help. But understanding will help more.

So yes.

I hope you finish this course knowing less than when you started.

Not because I want you to learn nothing. Not because facts do not matter. Not because research is optional. Not because communication theory is decorative academic confetti.

I hope you know less in the sense that I hope some of your easy certainty gets interrupted. I hope some of the standard social recipes stop feeling automatic. I hope you become less satisfied with advice that is technically clear but relationally thin. I hope you become more willing to ask what a situation depends on before reaching for the nearest rule.

Because if we want to improve our communicating, we have to change how we think and talk about communication. Otherwise, we stay stuck in the paradox: more advice, more tools, more scripts, more knowledge, and still not enough understanding.

See you in the mess.

Key Terms at a Glance

Communication paradox: The tension between how much attention, training, technology, and advice we spend on communication and how uncertain, anxious, or stuck people still feel when they actually have to communicate.

Propositional success: Succeeding with a claim: having a belief or statement that is accurate, reliable, and true.

Knowing: Having accurate information you can state, recall, identify, or correct.

Monologic thinking: Either/or thinking and one-answer thinking that pushes communication into binaries like right/wrong, true/false, good/bad, or victim/villain.

Certainty trap: The moment when feeling like we know makes us stop asking questions.

Brittle knowledge: Knowledge or advice that breaks when the context changes.

Conduit model: A way of imagining communication as messages moving through a channel from sender to receiver.

Objectual understanding: Understanding something as a connected whole by grasping how its parts, patterns, causes, and relationships fit together.

Understanding: Grasping how pieces of information connect so a person, situation, relationship, or interaction makes more sense.

Dialectical thinking: Both/and thinking that holds more than one truth at the same time.

Productive confusion: The discomfort that happens when an old certainty stops working before a new understanding has fully formed.

Sense-making: Figuring out what something means while you are still inside the interaction.

Communication myth: A repeated communication belief that contains a partial truth but becomes misleading when treated as universal or complete.

Heuristic: A flexible guide or tool for thinking, not a universal rule or script.

Parenthetical disambiguation: Using parentheses to slow down a familiar word and show that more than one meaning, assumption, or tension may be present.

Communication charlatans: People or platforms that sell easy communication certainty without the judgment needed to understand actual situations.

Final Reflections

Before moving on, take a minute. Think of one piece of communication advice you have heard before. Maybe: “Just communicate.” “Be direct.” “Don’t go to bed angry.” “Use I-statements.” “Listen more.” “Set boundaries.” “Never assume.”

Now ask: When might this advice help? When might it fail? What does it assume communication is? What would I need to understand before using it well?

Now try one more: think of a recent moment when you felt certain about what someone meant. What did you know? What did you assume? What else might have been true?

And one last one: where do you most want a communication rule right now? A friendship? A family relationship? A romantic relationship? A workplace situation? A group project? What makes that situation hard to understand?

Do not worry about having the perfect answer. That is not the goal. The goal is to start noticing the difference between knowing the advice and understanding the communicating.
