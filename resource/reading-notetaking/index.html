Resource: Reading and Research

Notes That Think

A field guide for reading research directly, using AI carefully, and turning notes into your own understanding.

Overview: Why We Still Read

We live in a moment when AI can summarize almost anything in seconds. That sounds convenient, and sometimes it is. But polished language is not the same thing as understanding. A summary can leave out the method, flatten the evidence, miss the caution, or invent a source that never existed.

That is why we still read important sources directly. We do not take notes because we are trying to make reading harder. We take notes because notes keep the work of understanding attached to us. As we read, we decide what matters, put ideas in our own words, connect the article to what we already know, and leave ourselves a trail we can use later when we write a summary.

AI should help us spend deeper time with the sources that matter most, not give us a reason to stop reading them.

AI Shortcuts Have Consequences

We do not have to be anti-AI to be honest about the risk. AI can take notes for us. It can summarize, outline, bullet, label, and reformat. What it cannot do is understand for us. Understanding happens when we connect new information to what we already know, test whether a claim makes sense, notice what confuses us, and decide what the evidence actually supports.

This connects back to the Chapter 0 difference between knowing and understanding. Knowing can sound like, "I have the answer." Understanding sounds more like, "I can explain how the pieces fit, why the claim matters, and where the limits are." AI can hand us a polished version of knowing. It cannot give us the lived mental work of building the pattern.

Lawyers have already learned this publicly. In Mata v. Avianca, attorneys filed legal cases generated by ChatGPT. The cases looked real enough to cite, but they were not real, and the court sanctioned the lawyers.8 In Indiana, a lawyer faced a recommended $15,000 sanction after briefs included AI-generated citations that did not exist.9 In New York, judges questioned attorneys on video after a brief cited fictitious cases and misrepresented others.10

What We Should Learn From Those Cases

AI can sound official while being wrong. Fake citations can arrive in fluent language, polished formatting, and confident tone.

Verification is still our job. If we repeat a claim, cite a source, or summarize an article, we need to know what the source actually says.

The learning research points in the same direction. Unguarded AI can help us complete a task while the tool is available but leave us weaker when we have to perform without it.11 In a reading-comprehension study, note-taking alone and note-taking with an LLM supported comprehension and retention better than LLM use alone.12 Convenience can feel like learning before it actually becomes learning.

Why this matters for us as readers

When we outsource the whole reading process, we also outsource the practice that builds judgment. AI can help us generate questions, preview terms, or test whether our summary makes sense. But if we never read the source and never make our own notes, we may end up with information we cannot explain.

A useful rule is this: let AI help around the reading, not replace the reading. We can ask it to define a term, give us questions to bring to the article, or compare our summary with the source after we have written one. But we still need to do the noticing, connecting, doubting, and explaining ourselves.

Why this article works for practice

What Your Notes Need To Catch

Your notes are for you. They do not need to look formal, perfect, or identical to anyone else's. They need to help you explain the article later in your own words. That means your notes should catch the structure of the article, not just the sentences that sounded important.

The five questions your notes should answer

What is this article trying to understand? How did the authors study it? What did they find? What do the findings mean? What is still limited, confusing, or worth questioning?

Write down the essentials

Authors, title, year, purpose, research question, key concepts, method, participants or data, findings, interpretation, limitations, and your questions.

Make the notes usable

Paraphrase. Draw arrows. Ask questions. Connect the article to class ideas and your prior knowledge. The point is not to make pretty notes. The point is to make notes that help you write.

Paraphrasing means translating, not swapping words

Definition: Put the idea into your own language so you can tell whether you actually understand it. Example: "The authors are not saying estrangement is always good. They are saying distance can sometimes function as protection, safety, or self-preservation."

Arrows show relationships

Definition: Use arrows to show how one idea leads to, complicates, supports, or limits another. Example: "professional discourse -> assumes estrangement is dysfunctional -> participants complicate that assumption."

Conversing means talking back to the article

Definition: Your margin notes can sound like your mind at work: "I wonder if this is related to family boundaries," "I hate this assumption," "This reminds me of Chapter 0," or "Wait, who gets to define healthy?"

Personal engagement deepens understanding

Definition: When we react, connect, question, and argue with the reading, the brain has more ways to attach the new information to existing knowledge. The goal is not fake objectivity in the margins. The goal is an honest record of how your understanding is forming.

Why personal engagement is not just a vibe

The human brain learns by building connections. We remember and understand more when we actively select information, explain it, connect it to prior knowledge, and monitor confusion. That is why a note like "I wonder if this connects to boundaries" can be more useful than a perfect copied sentence. It gives your brain a relationship to work with.

Handwriting and annotation may also bring sensorimotor, spatial, and memory cues into the task. Neuroscience studies suggest that handwriting and paper-based work can involve richer patterns of brain activity and retrieval cues than simple keyboarding or screen-based recording, though these studies should not be overstated as proof that paper is always better.13 The safest claim is still the most useful one: the method helps when it makes you think with the material.

Handwritten Notes: Slow Down To Think

Handwritten notes can help learning because they slow us down enough to choose, condense, and rephrase. The strongest research claim is not that paper has magic in it. The point is processing. When we write by hand, we usually cannot copy everything, so we have to decide what matters and translate it into our own words.7

That is exactly what we need when reading Allen and Moore. We are not trying to preserve every sentence. We are trying to build a working explanation: the article challenges the assumption that estrangement automatically equals dysfunction, uses interviews and iterative analysis, and asks us to think about who gets to define a functional family.

Research note: why handwriting often helps

Note-taking supports learning through encoding and storage: we learn while making the notes, and we benefit later when we review them.7 Studies on handwritten versus typed notes suggest that handwriting can encourage deeper processing because it discourages verbatim transcription, though the evidence is mixed and the best claim is modest rather than absolute.7 In other words: handwriting works best when it makes us select, paraphrase, organize, and review.

Digital Notes: Organize And Revisit

Digital notes can work beautifully when we use them to organize thinking. They are searchable, easy to revise, accessible across devices, and useful when we want tags, links, or shared notes. The risk is speed. If we type too quickly, we may copy the article without actually processing it.

For this article, a good digital note does not just say, "functional family estrangement." It explains what the concept does: it challenges a common family norm by showing that distance can sometimes protect well-being.

Use labels so the notes have a job

Try labels such as purpose, method, finding, quote to revisit, my question, and summary sentence. Labels keep digital notes from becoming a giant pasteboard.

Digital notes are especially useful after reading. You can revise your first impressions, group related ideas, and turn scattered notes into a summary paragraph.

Handwritten Annotations: Talk To The Page

Annotation is not just highlighting. Good annotation is a conversation with the text. We mark a phrase, then we say why it matters, what it makes us think of, what it connects to, or what we still do not understand.

Annotations do not have to sound formal. They should sound like thinking in motion: "This changes my assumption," "method clue," "connects to family norms," "is this about safety or avoidance?" Those notes matter because they connect your existing knowledge to the article you are reading now.

Digital Annotations: Label Your Thinking

Digital annotation works best when every comment has a purpose. If every note says "important," future-you will not know what was important or why. Instead, name the move: claim, evidence, method clue, question, connection, confusion, or summary seed.

Digital annotation can also help us return to earlier thinking and revise it. Research on online annotation suggests that annotation becomes stronger when it supports review, questioning, summarizing, and sometimes collaboration.6

Creative Notes: Build The Summary

Your notes are not the final product. They are the thinking space. You can use a concept map, mind map, two-column response chart, research flowchart, article anatomy diagram, key-term web, storyboard, or whatever structure helps you explain the article later.

Creative does not mean random. The structure should help you move from scattered ideas to a paragraph summary. You are trying to see how the purpose, method, finding, interpretation, and limits fit together.

Click the visual-to-paragraph image to enlarge it.

The visual-to-paragraph move

First, we make the article visible: purpose, method, finding, so what, and limit. Then we choose the strongest pieces and turn them into sentences. A good paragraph does not list every note. It explains the article's logic.

Use the structure that helps you write. If a concept map helps, use it. If two messy columns help, use those. The notes belong to you.

Sources: The Receipts Behind The Warning

Your Brain Cannot Outsource Understanding

Research on note-taking, annotation, generative learning, and cognitive offloading all points toward the same practical warning: a tool can store information for us, but it cannot build our understanding for us. Understanding depends on mental work such as selecting what matters, connecting it to what we already know, checking confusion, paraphrasing, and deciding what evidence supports.

The neuroscience research is suggestive, not magical. Some studies find that handwriting or paper-based work may recruit richer visual, motor, spatial, and memory-related processes than typing or mobile-device recording.13 But the point is not "paper good, digital bad." The point is that learning needs active engagement. If AI takes notes and we never do the thinking, the page may look finished while our understanding is still unfinished.
