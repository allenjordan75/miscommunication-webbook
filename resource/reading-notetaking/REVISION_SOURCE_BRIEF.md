# Reading Note-Taking Resource: Revision Source Brief

Use this brief to guide future revisions of `resource/reading-notetaking/index.html`.

## Core Direction

This resource should teach students how to move from a dense social scientific research article to organized notes and then to a concise paragraph summary. It should avoid assignment logistics, grading language, point values, and submission instructions.

The core argument everything should organize around:

The presence of AI does not mean students should stop reading sources directly or stop taking notes. AI means students should get better at deciding which sources matter most, then spend deeper time reading, annotating, checking, and understanding those sources.

AI can help students orient, ask better questions, define unfamiliar terms, generate practice prompts, and test their explanations. It should not replace direct engagement with the original source. The resource should make this distinction repeatedly: AI can support inquiry, but it cannot do the reader's responsibility for them.

The central teaching move:

Visual notes are the thinking space. The paragraph is the synthesis space.

Good reading notes do not prove that students read the article. They help readers explain the article. The best notes make visible what the article is trying to do, how the authors tried to answer their question, what they found, what they think it means, and what remains unclear.

## Design Anchor

Warm, readable, student-facing, slightly playful, and still academic.

For the current zine-insert direction, keep the page visually alive but credible:

- Use page-like blocks, layered paper, highlighter accents, article artifacts, margin-note energy, and bold section titles.
- Avoid making the page feel like a generic dashboard, worksheet packet, or AI-generated study blog.
- Prefer fewer larger editorial spreads over many equal cards.
- Keep examples anchored in the Allen and Moore article.
- Make clickable images visually obvious and accessible.

## AI-Era Rationale

The resource should justify notes as an AI-era literacy practice. AI can summarize quickly, but a summary does not prove understanding. Students still need to evaluate where claims come from, how evidence was produced, what the study can and cannot show, and whether a fluent explanation is grounded in reliable research.

Future revisions should connect note-taking to three overlapping literacies:

Information literacy:
Students need to recognize information needs, locate and evaluate sources, understand how information is produced, and use information ethically and reflectively. Information is not merely retrieved; it is created, circulated, interpreted, and evaluated.

Scientific literacy:
Students need to understand scientific processes, interpret evidence, read scientific texts, evaluate claims, and apply scientific reasoning to public and personal decisions. Reading and writing are central to scientific literacy, not add-ons.

AI literacy:
Students need to understand, use, question, and evaluate AI systems and outputs. Fluent language is not the same thing as evidence. AI should support inquiry, not replace interpretation.

Possible student-facing claim:

AI can give you a polished answer. Notes help you check whether the answer deserves your trust.

Another student-facing claim:

AI should free you to spend more time with the sources that matter most, not give you an excuse to stop reading them.

Use real professional failures as concrete examples. Legal examples are especially effective because lawyers have been sanctioned for citing AI-generated cases that did not exist. The teaching point is not "AI is bad." The teaching point is that professionals still have to verify, read, and understand the sources they use.

## Required Content For Reading Notes

Any visual or digital note format should still capture:

- Article identity: authors, year, title, journal/source, topic area.
- Overall purpose: what the article tries to understand, explain, test, complicate, or critique.
- Main research question/problem: the puzzle, gap, tension, or question that organizes the study.
- Key terms and concepts: what they mean in the article and why they matter.
- Background/literature gap: what prior work suggests and what remains missing.
- Method: study type, participants/sample/texts, data, collection process, analysis process, and why the method fits the question.
- Results/findings: the major pattern, theme, relationship, difference, or answer.
- Interpretation/so what: what the authors think the findings mean.
- Limits/cautions/complications: what the article cannot fully answer and what should not be overclaimed.
- Specific confusion/questions: precise points of uncertainty, not just "I am confused."
- One-sentence gist: a sentence that captures argument, method, and significance.
- Paragraph summary: a concise synthesis in prose.

Paragraph frame:

In this article, [authors] examine [topic/problem/question]. The purpose of the article is to understand [overall purpose], especially in relation to [key concept or issue]. To answer this question, the authors [describe method/data in plain language]. They found that [main finding or findings]. The authors interpret these findings to mean [so what/implication]. One limitation, complication, or remaining question is [limit/confusion/question].

## Visual Note Structures To Preserve Or Add

The page does not need to show all of these at once, but future revisions can draw from them:

- Research flowchart: problem/gap -> question -> method/data -> findings -> so what -> still wondering.
- Mind map: main question in center with branches for terms, method, findings, limits, questions, and connection.
- Concept map with linking verbs: nodes connected by labels such as complicates, supports, challenges, depends on, limits.
- Article anatomy diagram: purpose, question, key concept, method, evidence, interpretation, limits, confusion.
- Research house/article architecture: foundation, door, rooms, roof, fence, porch light.
- Claim-evidence-method-limit chart.
- Methods lens diagram: inside the lens = what the method makes visible; outside = what it may miss.
- Results tree.
- Research detective board.
- Two-voice notes.
- Commonsense/uncommonsense turn chart.
- One-page research poster notes.
- Three-pass reading notes.

## Existing Example Article

Use Allen and Moore's article as the shared example:

Allen, J., & Moore, J. (2017). Troubling the functional/dysfunctional family binary through the articulation of functional family estrangement. Western Journal of Communication, 81(3), 281-299.

Use this article to model:

- Purpose: trouble the functional/dysfunctional family binary.
- Method: critical-qualitative analysis using interviews, professional discourse, and Butler/performativity.
- Finding: some estranged participants frame distance as functional, positive, workable, or healthier.
- Interpretation: family health is produced through discourse, not simply discovered.
- Question: when does encouraging communication become pressure to maintain harmful closeness?

## Voice Rules

Keep the voice student-facing, active, warm, and direct.

Prefer:

- "AI can summarize quickly, but a summary does not prove understanding."
- "Your notes give you a trail of judgment."
- "Good notes help you explain the article."
- "Confusion belongs in the notes when you name it precisely."

Avoid:

- "Student payoff"
- overly corporate labels
- generic AI-sounding transitions
- too many parallel lists in the page itself
- claiming that handwriting is always better than typing
- claiming that AI is always bad or useless
