# Textbook Guide

This is the standing memory file for drafting future chapters of **(Mis)Communication: An (Un)Commonsense Approach to Interpersonal Communication**. Every chapter packet and section draft should use this guide before generating prose.

## Source Models

- **Chapter 0 is the voice model.** Use it to preserve the author's student-facing, warm, rigorous, conversational style.
- **Chapter 2 is the structure model for subsequent chapters.** Future chapter reading canvases should follow the Chapter 2 pattern unless the author explicitly changes it.

## Book Purpose

The textbook helps first-year interpersonal communication students move from memorizing communication knowledge toward practicing communication understanding. It does not reject concepts, research, vocabulary, or advice. It asks students to treat those things as tools for noticing, questioning, and making sense of actual interactions.

The book's recurring move is:

1. Start with the common-sense way a topic is usually understood.
2. Identify what that common-sense framing helps students see.
3. Show what it hides, flattens, or makes too certain.
4. Offer an (un)commonsense approach that gives students better questions, more context, and more responsibility.
5. End with practical, concrete ways to notice and respond differently.

## Required Terminology

Use these terms exactly unless the author asks for a global terminology revision:

- the common-sense approach
- the (un)commonsense approach
- knowing
- understanding
- knowing vs. understanding
- interpersonal communicating
- sense-making
- commonsensical knowledge
- communication myth
- heuristic

## Terms To Avoid

Do not use these shortcuts:

- common-sense side
- uncommon side
- uncommon-sense side
- common-sense view
- uncommon-sense approach
- communication as just sending messages
- communication as simply transfer

If a nearby sentence needs variety, rewrite the sentence instead of inventing shorthand for the key terms.

## Framework Salience Rules

### Always Available

These can shape any chapter, but they should appear only where useful:

- knowing vs. understanding
- common-sense approach vs. (un)commonsense approach
- advice as heuristic rather than universal rule
- communication myths as partial truths that become misleading when treated as complete
- context, relationship, history, power, emotion, body, culture, technology, and situation

### Use Carefully

These are foundational but should not be re-explained in every chapter:

- the communication paradox
- propositional success
- brittle certainty
- monologic thinking
- parenthetical disambiguation
- communication charlatans

### Default Rule

Do not force Chapter 0 concepts into later chapters. A future chapter should sound like it remembers Chapter 0, not like it is rewriting Chapter 0.

## Chapter 2 Structure For Future Chapters

Each subsequent chapter reading canvas should use this structure:

1. **Chapter Header**
   - Chapter number or chapter label
   - Chapter title
   - Subtitle that names the central shift

2. **Before You Read**
   - One paragraph that introduces the topic through a common-sense framing.
   - One paragraph or embedded contrast that names the (un)commonsense reframing.
   - A **Big Idea** callout that gives the chapter's main claim in student-facing language.

3. **Read For These Moves**
   - Four to six moves students should track.
   - Each move should have a short bold label and one concrete sentence explaining what to notice.
   - At least one move should explicitly compare the common-sense and (un)commonsense approaches.

4. **The Three Named Leaps**
   - Exactly three named leaps unless the author approves a different number.
   - Each leap should be phrased as **From X to Y**.
   - Each leap should explain what changes in how students think about the chapter topic.

5. **Key Terms To Track**
   - Five to eight key terms.
   - Each term should be defined in plain language.
   - Definitions should connect to the chapter's conceptual argument, not just dictionary meaning.

6. **After You Read**
   - One reflective prompt that asks students to apply the chapter to a concrete interaction.
   - A **Quick Check** callout that asks students to explain the central difference the chapter makes.

## Chapter 0 Voice Rules

The voice should feel like a thoughtful professor talking to students without pretending human communication is tidier than it is.

Use:

- Direct openings that create a puzzle or productive discomfort.
- Short clarifying sentences after complex claims.
- Concrete everyday examples: texts, friendships, families, roommates, group projects, work, apologies, silence, conflict, ambiguity.
- "The problem is not X. The problem is Y." structures.
- "Not because... but because..." structures.
- Questions that slow down certainty: "Clear to whom?" "Under what conditions?" "What does this situation need?"
- Gentle humor when it sharpens the point.
- Both/and thinking: concepts can be useful and limited.
- Plain-language translation after technical concepts.
- Student-facing metacommentary: "This matters because..." "In simpler language..." "So here is the problem..."

Avoid:

- Corporate polish.
- Generic textbook neutrality.
- Generic AI prose or vague filler claims.
- Grand claims without examples.
- Long abstract explanation before students know why it matters.
- Over-explaining Chapter 0 in every later chapter.
- Treating research as decoration.
- Treating advice as a universal rule.
- Making the (un)commonsense approach sound like the common-sense approach plus nicer language.
- Favoring style, rhythm, or cleverness over the author-provided content in the worksheet.
- Overusing single-sentence paragraphs for emphasis.
- Using "this," "that," "it," or "the problem" when the referent is unclear.

## Prose Clarity And Source-Specific Drafting Rules

- Use the opening narrative, preferred language, and revised-language boxes as controlling content when they are present. Do not replace the author's narrative with a looser paraphrase unless the author asks for a rewrite.
- After a long opening narrative, add a clear transition header that tells students the chapter is moving from story into chapter preview, overview, or foundations.
- Use subheaders between major ideas inside each main section so students can see the chapter's logic while reading.
- Bold key terms when they are introduced and when emphasis helps students track the chapter's vocabulary.
- Make pronoun reference explicit. Revise sentences that use "this," "that," "it," or "the problem" without a clear noun nearby.
- Keep informal voice concentrated in the opening narrative and occasional author-note moments. After the introduction, let the tone become more explanatory, grounded, and content-forward.
- Overview or foundations sections should establish the topic, stakes, definitions, and needed background before leaning heavily on the common-sense/(un)commonsense contrast.
- When the chapter is about peer-reviewed research, use **peer-reviewed research** or **peer-reviewed article** when that is the actual object of discussion. Do not slide into generic "research" if the distinction matters.
- Do not add a "how claims travel," "how research travels," circulation, pipeline, press-release, headline, or social-media distortion framing unless the worksheet explicitly asks for that framing. If related sources appear in the bibliography, use them only where the worksheet gives them a clear job.
- For Chapter 3-style research sections, prefer phrasing such as "The common-sense approach approaches peer-reviewed research as..." over detached constructions such as "From the common-sense approach, research is..." so the sentence names the approach as the active interpretive frame.

## Paragraph Pattern

A strong paragraph usually does one of these jobs:

- Names a familiar assumption.
- Complicates that assumption.
- Gives a concrete example.
- Translates a research/theory concept into student language.
- Shows what changes if students use the (un)commonsense approach.
- Asks a better question.

If a paragraph does none of those jobs, revise or cut it.

## Research Use

Research should be used specifically. Do not write "research shows" unless the packet names the study, concept, finding, or source.

When source support is weak, say so before drafting. Do not create scholarly confidence out of source fog.

## Example Requirement

Every major claim needs at least one concrete demonstration. The example can be:

- a student example
- a roommate example
- a friendship example
- a family example
- a dating or romantic relationship example
- a workplace example
- a media/technology example
- a research example
- a brief hypothetical

Examples should help students understand the concept, not merely decorate the page.

## AI Role

AI should act as a developmental editor and drafting assistant. It should not become the author.

Before drafting, AI must identify:

- unsupported claims
- missing examples
- unclear source relationships
- terminology risks
- framework overuse
- places where Chapter 0 concepts are being forced
- places where the Chapter 2 structure is incomplete

If the packet does not contain enough information, ask specific questions instead of guessing.
