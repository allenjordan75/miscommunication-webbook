Chapter 2 Reading

Perception (Mis)Perception

From Accuracy to Sense-making

Start Here

This chapter asks why perception feels so obvious even when people honestly perceive the same situation differently.

Main Shift

Move from asking only whose perception is accurate toward asking how each perception became meaningful.

Learning Goals

  1. Explain why The Dress is a perception problem, not only a color debate.
  2. Define perception as selection, organization, interpretation, and storying.
  3. Compare the common sense and (un)common sense approaches to perception.
  4. Describe three discontinuous leaps: linear processing to recursive sense-making, private mind to collaborative sense-making, and bias as error to monitored blind spot.
  5. Practice shifting from accuracy-only reactions toward perspective-taking, productive uncertainty, and more careful communicating.

Reading Path

1. The Dress

Notice how quickly perceptual difference turns into certainty, disbelief, and accusation.

2. Perception

Track the four processes: selection, organization, interpretation, and storying.

3. Approaches

Compare perception as accurate representation with perception as situated sense-making.

4. Leaps

Look for the three shifts: recursive loop, collaborative sense-making, and monitored blind spot.

5. Practice

Use Bob and Joan to practice moving accuracy out of the center without abandoning it.

6. Closing

Carry forward the sentence: “This is how I see it, and I know that is not the whole world.”

Key Terms To Watch

Perception, selection, attention, salience, organization, Gestalt principles, McGurk effect, interpretation, attribution, labeling, storying, common sense approach to perception, (un)common sense approach to perception, naive realism, confirmation bias, reconstructive memory, open perceptual feedback loop, distributed cognition, shared attention, bias, bias blind spot, perspective-taking, dialectical thinking, and productive uncertainty.

Pause And Respond

  1. After The Dress: When have you been sure your perception was obvious, only to learn someone else perceived the situation differently?
  2. After Selection: What cue do you tend to notice first in conflict: tone, timing, wording, facial expression, silence, or something else?
  3. After Organization: How can the same cues be grouped into different patterns?
  4. After Interpretation: What label changes the response: “busy,” “rude,” “anxious,” “avoidant,” “direct,” or “careful”?
  5. After Leaps: What does your position make easy to see, and what might it hide?
  6. After Practice: Rewrite Bob’s reaction so it keeps accuracy in the room without letting accuracy run the whole room.
